## What are HR recertification credits?

HR recertification credits are the currency HR professionals use to keep a certification active after earning them. Every major credentialing body issues its own version, under its own name, but the mechanics are similar: complete a pre-approved learning activity, log the hours or points it’s worth, and submit them before your certification cycle ends.

The terminology differs by body. SHRM calls its credits professional development credits (PDCs), while HRCI simply calls them recertification credits and ATD awards points toward its APTD and CPTD credentials.

HRPA and CPHR both track continuing professional development (CPD) hours in Canada, as do IHRP in Singapore and SABPP in South Africa. IHRIM’s HRIP credential runs on recertification credit hours (RCH). Despite different labels, they’re based on the same underlying idea: proof that you kept learning after you passed the exam.

What doesn’t change across these systems is the pre-approval requirement. A course must be submitted to and accepted by your specific credentialing body before the hours you spend on it count toward your total. General “CE eligible” language on a course page isn’t the same as confirmed pre-approval with your body, so it’s worth checking before you enroll rather than after.



## How many credits do you need for recertification?

To keep your SHRM-CP or SHRM-SCP active, you need 60 SHRM PDCs every three years. HRCI recertification runs on the same three-year cycle for the PHR and PHRi, which also require 60 credits.

Some HRCI credentials include extra category rules. The SPHR, GPHR, and PHRca require 15 of your 60 credits in a specific area: business strategy for SPHR, global HR for GPHR, and California law for PHRca. The remaining 45 can come from general HR activities. Every HRCI cycle also needs one ethics activity, which counts toward your 60 credits.

Credit requirements outside SHRM and HRCI follow the same three-year logic, just with different totals. ATD requires 40 points over three years for the APTD and 60 for the CPTD. HRPA asks its CHRP, CHRL, and CHRE members for 66.67 CPD hours every three years, and CPHR designations across Canada generally follow a similar structure.

IHRIM (HRIP), IHRP in Singapore, and SABPP in South Africa each run their own CPD or RCH systems, so if you hold one of those credentials, check your specific body’s portal rather than assuming SHRM or HRCI’s numbers apply.

As a rule of thumb, one hour of qualifying learning time usually equals one credit, often rounded to the nearest quarter hour. That means a 30-hour program and six five-hour webinars can close the same credit gap, though they’re likely very different in practice.

Six shorter webinars could get you there, too, but you’d need six sign-ups and six certificates.

## Other ways to earn HR recertification credits

Courses aren’t the only way to earn recertification credit. Most associations also recognize live events, in-house training, and the work you do on the job, provided the activity is documented and comes from an approved source.

### Live events and webinars

Some HR associations award recertification credit for attending a live event or webinar, which is separate from enrolling in a full course. SHRM, for example, credits in-person and virtual events like conferences, seminars, and workshops under its Advance Your Education category.

AIHR’s live events also count towards recertification. As a SHRM recertification provider, each AIHR live event you attend is worth 1 PDC, which adds up steadily if you’re a Full Academy Access member attending them regularly.

Shorter webinars and e-learning courses can still help if you’re only a few credits short. HRCI, for example, awards one credit hour for every hour of qualifying interactive e-learning, and calculates conference and seminar credit based only on educational time. This makes short courses useful for closing small credit gaps, but you should track activity IDs, certificates, and reporting rules carefully.

### In-house training

Many credentialing bodies accept the structured training your own organization delivers, whether it’s an internal workshop, a recurring lunch-and-learn series, or a formal program you help build for your HR team .

SHRM and HRCI generally allow in-house training to count in their general education categories, as long as it’s HR-relevant, and you can document what it covered and how long it ran. If your company uses outside training, an AIHR Boot Camp or Team License , for instance, counts the same way a recertification course would, provided your credentialing body recognizes the provider.

### On-the-job experience

For some associations, on-the-job experience counts too. SHRM’s Advance Your Organization category lets you earn PDCs for leading or managing an HR project at your organization, from roughly 10 PDCs for 40-plus hours of project work up to 30 PDCs for 120-plus hours, capped at 30 PDCs per cycle.

This is where the skills from an AIHR Certificate Program tend to pay off twice. After getting your certificate and earning recertification credits, you can apply what you’ve learned directly to a project at work to earn even more credits.

## How to choose an HR recertification course worth your time

Credit eligibility is the starting point, but shouldn’t determine your whole decision. Once you know a course counts, treat it like any other investment in your career .

### Confirm that the course counts toward your credential

Before you pay for a course, check that it counts toward your specific credential. Don’t rely on a vague “recertification eligible” label, but look for the exact credentialing body, the number of credits you’ll earn, and any category rules that apply. For example, some HRCI credentials need credits in business strategy, global HR , or California law. If the provider’s page doesn’t make this clear, ask before you enroll.

### Check how many HR bodies recognize the same hours

If you hold more than one credential, choose a course that can support more than one recertification requirement. AIHR’s Certificate Programs are recognized by HRCI, SHRM, HRPA, and CPHR, so a single program can often support multiple credentials at once. That helps if you already hold two credentials, or are working toward a second one while maintaining the first.

### Look for practical skill-building, not just credits

A 45-minute webinar can earn you recertification credits, but it may not provide the same value. While a certificate program is typically much more in-depth than a webinar, as it teaches you practical HR skills you can apply on the job immediately, or discuss in an interview, performance review, or team meeting.

### Compare the cost per credit

Cost per credit hour can help you compare options more fairly. A $1,000 program that earns 30-plus credits across several associations may cost less per credit than a $300 webinar worth two credits. While the program costs more upfront, but it gives you greater value for money if it covers several requirements and builds a skill you need.

### Choose a format you’ll actually finish

Self-paced online courses, like AIHR’s Certificate Programs, work well if you need to study around a full-time HR job . Live, instructor-led courses can benefit you if you need discussion, structure, and a fixed deadline. Choose the format that fits your schedule and learning style.

### Check how you’ll report your credits

Finally, check how the credits are reported. Some providers submit your completion directly to HRCI or SHRM, while others give you a certificate and expect you to log the activity yourself. Each approach can work, but if you have to self-report, be sure this step to your calendar as soon as you finish the course, so you’ll report your credits on time.
