# 150 Employee Evaluation Examples & Comments for Every Rating

Strong employee evaluation examples can help you turn vague performance review comments into clear, useful feedback. These detail what an employee has done, the impact it’s had on their team and department, and what should happen next. But this may not be the norm right now. In fact, according to Gallup , only 16% of employees found their last conversation with their managers meaningful.

If you’re an HR Manager , it’s vital to be able to write specific and fair evaluations. This article details how an employee evaluation can be effective, and what to do and not do when conducting one. It also provides 150 employee evaluation examples you can use, and explains how you can adapt them to suit your teams.

## What makes an employee evaluation effective?

An effective and useful employee evaluation answers three simple questions:

- What was the specific performance?

- What impact did it have?

- What should happen next?

Strong employee evaluations focus on observable behavior or measurable outcomes . For instance, compare “has a good attitude” with “kept stakeholders informed when project priorities changed”. Unlike the first comment, the second one actually tells the employee exactly what they did and why it helped.

Growth feedback needs the same level of clarity. “Needs to improve communication ” gives an employee little to work with, but “would benefit from sharing project risks earlier, before deadlines are affected” points to a specific change to work toward.

Your evaluation should also balance strengths with areas for growth. Even when performance falls short, the goal isn’t to list faults but to create a clear, evidence-based picture of performance and a practical path forward. ## 150 employee evaluation examples HR managers can use

#### Exceeds/meets expectations

- Communicates project priorities clearly across cross-functional teams .

- Shares concise updates that keep stakeholders aligned.

- Explains complex issues in language that clients can easily understand.

- Flags risks before they affect project timelines.

- Tailors communication style to different audiences.

- Keeps meetings focused on decisions, actions, and owners.

- Responds promptly to important stakeholder questions and concerns.

- Documents key decisions so teams can move forward.

- Gives colleagues clear context when handing off work.

#### Needs improvement

- Would benefit from sharing project updates earlier with stakeholders.

- Could make written updates more concise and action-oriented.

- Often leaves important questions unanswered beyond agreed response times.

- Has improved clarity but still misses key details.

- Needs to confirm understanding before moving forward on complex requests.

- Needs to communicate potential risks before deadlines become difficult.

- Would benefit from organizing presentations around clearer takeaways.

- Sometimes provides too much detail without identifying priorities.

- Should adapt communication style more consistently for senior stakeholders.

- Needs to document important decisions more consistently after meetings.

## How to adapt these examples for your team

If you simply copy the examples word-for-word, it won’t create a strong employee evaluation. The real value comes from using the structure and replacing generic language with evidence.

Use these four HR rules to help guide you:

- Add a real project, metric , or outcome. Replace “project deadlines” with the actual initiative, timeline, or metric. If the employee reduced processing time by 15%, say so.

- Match the comment to the role. A Recruiter , HR Business Partner , Payroll Specialist , and People Manager won’t show performance in the same way.

- Check the evidence. Make sure every comment reflects something that happened during the review period.

- Keep the language consistent. Managers should use similar standards when describing comparable performance levels.

Consistency is especially important. Inconsistent performance review language can quickly create perceptions of favoritism . Calling one employee’s missed deadline a “development opportunity” but labeling another employee “unreliable” for the same mistake may create distrust and resentment among team members, and in doing so, hurt team morale .

A consistent framework doesn’t mean every employee receives identical comments. It means you evaluate comparable behaviors against clear expectations and support their judgments with evidence. For a broader view of process design, see CIPD’s guide to effective performance management .

## HR’s dos and don’ts for employee evaluations

Here are some key dos and don’ts to help you provide better employee evaluations:

Dos

Dont’s

Back every rating with a specific example or metric from the review period.

Let recency bias drive the appraisal; review evidence from the full period.

Ask for the employee’s self-evaluation before finalizing comments.

Compare the employee’s performance with a named colleague.

Pair every growth area with a genuine strength.

Use “good job” or “needs improvement” without supporting evidence.

Describe behavior, impact, and the expected next step.

Label personality traits such as “lazy”, “difficult”, or “unmotivated”.

Use role-specific metrics where they genuinely measure performance.

Treat every metric as meaningful without considering role context.

Review evidence across the entire appraisal period.

Let one recent success or mistake dominate the evaluation.

Invite employees to challenge or clarify the evidence respectfully.

Treat the manager’s perspective as the only valid account.

Connect development feedback to a concrete action or goal.

Identify a weakness without explaining how the employee can improve.

Input from an employee’s self-evaluation makes the process more reciprocal by giving them space to share their perspective and feel heard. It can also reveal achievements you may have missed, explain the obstacles behind a result, and give the employee more ownership of their development .

It’s also important not to make a yearly, bi-annual, or quarterly performance appraisal the only time employees receive feedback. Instead, you should provide it throughout the performance year, formally and informally. Employees shouldn’t be hearing crucial feedback for the first time during their evaluations.
